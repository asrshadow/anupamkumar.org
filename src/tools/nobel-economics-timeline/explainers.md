# Explainers — one per prize year

These are the site's own plain-English summaries of each prize. They are NOT part of the
dataset, and the tool labels them "Written for this site", separate from the official citation.

HOW TO EDIT
- Each year starts with a line that is exactly "## " followed by the four-digit year.
- Everything under that line, up to the next "## ", is that year's explainer.
- A blank line starts a new paragraph. Plain text only: no links, no bold, no italics.
- Use curly quotation marks (“ ” and ’) in the text, to match the rest of the site.
- Anything above the first "## " line (this note) is ignored by the tool.
- Save, and the timeline shows the change on the next build. Nothing else needs editing.

## 1969

The first economics prize went to the two people who did most to turn economics into a measuring science. Ragnar Frisch coined the word “econometrics” — the use of statistics to test economic ideas against data — and built mathematical models of how an economy moves over time. Jan Tinbergen built the first models of entire national economies, for the Netherlands and the United States, and showed that a government needs at least as many separate policy tools as it has goals.

## 1970

Paul Samuelson rebuilt much of economics on a common mathematical foundation, showing that problems as different as consumer choice, production and trade share the same underlying logic. His textbook, Economics, taught the subject to generations of students. His name is attached to results across the field, from how international trade affects wages to why public goods — things like street lighting that no one can be excluded from — tend to be underprovided by markets.

## 1971

Simon Kuznets did much of the pioneering work behind national income accounts, the system that produces GDP figures. He then used long runs of historical data to describe how economies change as they grow: people move from farming into industry and services, and inequality, he suggested, first rises and then falls as incomes increase — an idea now known as the Kuznets curve.

## 1972

John Hicks and Kenneth Arrow were honoured for work on general equilibrium — how prices across all markets settle at once, rather than one market at a time — and on welfare economics, which asks when an economic outcome is good for society. Hicks’s IS-LM diagram became the standard way of teaching how interest rates and output interact. Arrow proved that no voting system can turn individual preferences into a consistent social ranking while meeting a short list of reasonable fairness conditions, a result known as Arrow’s impossibility theorem.

## 1973

Wassily Leontief invented the input-output table: a grid recording how much each industry buys from every other industry to produce its output. With it, one can trace how a change in demand in one sector ripples through the whole economy. Input-output tables remain a core part of national accounts today, including India’s supply and use tables.

## 1974

A deliberately contrasting pair. Gunnar Myrdal and Friedrich von Hayek both did early work on money and business cycles, but drew opposite conclusions about the role of the state. Myrdal argued for active planning and the welfare state, and wrote a landmark study of race in the United States. Hayek argued that prices carry knowledge scattered across millions of people that no central planner could ever gather, which made him the leading critic of planned economies.

## 1975

Leonid Kantorovich and Tjalling Koopmans worked out, independently, on either side of the Iron Curtain, how to allocate limited resources in the best possible way. Their method, now called linear programming, finds the best combination of inputs when there are many constraints at once — how to route shipments, schedule machines or plan production. Kantorovich developed it in 1939 for a Soviet plywood trust. Koopmans developed it for wartime shipping.

## 1976

Milton Friedman argued that households base their spending on the income they expect over the long run, not on what they earn this year — the “permanent income” hypothesis. With Anna Schwartz he wrote a monetary history of the United States arguing that the Federal Reserve’s failure to stop the money supply collapsing turned a recession into the Great Depression. He also argued that there is no lasting trade-off between inflation and unemployment, and that policy works with long and unpredictable delays.

## 1977

Bertil Ohlin explained what a country trades by what it has in abundance: countries rich in labour export goods that need a lot of labour, and countries rich in capital export goods that need a lot of capital. James Meade showed how a government in an open economy can pursue two goals at once — full employment at home and a sustainable balance of payments with the rest of the world — and why each needs its own policy tool.

## 1978

Herbert Simon challenged the assumption that people and organisations make perfectly rational choices. Real decision-makers have limited time, information and attention, so they rely on rules of thumb and settle for an option that is good enough — what he called “satisficing”. His idea of “bounded rationality” came from studying how decisions are actually made inside firms and public administrations.

## 1979

The first prize for development economics. Arthur Lewis described how a poor economy grows as workers move from low-productivity farming into modern industry, with wages staying low until that surplus of rural labour runs out — the “Lewis turning point”. Theodore Schultz argued that traditional farmers are poor but not inefficient, and that the way out of poverty lies in investing in people — their education and health, which he called human capital — and in agricultural research.

## 1980

Lawrence Klein built large statistical models of whole economies, made up of hundreds of equations, which governments and businesses used to forecast growth and to test what a policy change would do before trying it. He also led Project LINK, which connected the models of many countries so that the effects of a shock could be traced across borders.

## 1981

James Tobin studied how people choose a mix of assets to balance return against risk, and how financial markets carry the effects of monetary policy through to spending, jobs and prices. His “q” ratio — the market value of a firm compared with the cost of replacing its capital — explains when firms choose to invest.

## 1982

George Stigler studied how industries are structured and how markets work when information is costly to find. He is best known for the theory of regulatory capture: regulation is often shaped by, and serves, the very industry it is meant to control, rather than the public.

## 1983

Gerard Debreu put general equilibrium theory on rigorous mathematical foundations. With Kenneth Arrow, he proved that, under clearly stated conditions, a set of prices exists at which every market in an economy clears at the same time. His short book Theory of Value set the standard of proof that economic theory has followed since.

## 1984

Richard Stone designed the system of national accounts — the double-entry framework that records what a country produces, earns and spends, and how those three totals must agree. It became the basis of the United Nations System of National Accounts, so every official GDP figure published today, India’s included, rests on his work.

## 1985

Franco Modigliani showed that people save while they work and run those savings down in retirement — the “life-cycle” hypothesis — so a country’s saving rate depends on its age structure and growth rate. With Merton Miller he also showed that, under idealised conditions, a firm’s value does not depend on whether it is financed by debt or by shares; real-world departures from that result, such as taxes, explain what actually happens.

## 1986

James Buchanan founded public choice theory, which applies the methods of economics to politics. Voters, politicians and officials are treated as people pursuing their own interests, not as selfless servants of the public good. He argued that better outcomes come from better rules — constitutions and fiscal rules that constrain what governments can do — rather than from hoping for better people in office.

## 1987

Robert Solow showed that, in the long run, rising living standards cannot come from simply piling up more machines, because each extra machine adds less than the last. Sustained growth in output per worker has to come from technological progress. When he measured the sources of American growth, most of it could not be explained by more capital or more labour; that unexplained part is still called the “Solow residual”.

## 1988

Maurice Allais, a French engineer turned economist, worked out the conditions under which markets use resources efficiently, and applied them to the pricing of public enterprises such as utilities. He is also known for the Allais paradox, which showed that people’s choices when facing risk regularly break the rules of the standard theory of rational choice.

## 1989

Trygve Haavelmo set econometrics on the foundations of probability theory: economic data should be treated as the outcome of a random process, which is what makes statistical testing legitimate. He also showed that because prices and quantities are determined together by supply and demand, simply relating one to the other with standard statistical methods gives misleading answers — the problem of “simultaneous equations”.

## 1990

Three founders of financial economics. Harry Markowitz showed how holding a varied mix of assets — diversification — reduces risk for the same expected return. William Sharpe built on that to show that an asset’s expected return depends on how much it moves with the market as a whole, a measure called “beta”. Merton Miller, with Franco Modigliani, showed how a firm’s choice between borrowing and issuing shares affects its value.

## 1991

Ronald Coase asked why firms exist at all if markets are efficient. His answer was transaction costs: finding partners, negotiating and enforcing contracts all cost something, and sometimes it is cheaper to organise activity inside a firm. He also showed that if property rights are clear and bargaining is cheap, people can settle problems like pollution between themselves — which means that, in the real world where bargaining is costly, the law and institutions matter a great deal.

## 1992

Gary Becker applied the economic way of thinking to parts of life that had been left to sociology and other disciplines: discrimination, crime, marriage and family, and education. His treatment of schooling and training as an investment that pays off over a working life, called human capital, is now central to how governments think about education.

## 1993

Robert Fogel and Douglass North brought economic theory and statistics to the study of history. Fogel estimated that railways added far less to American growth than had been assumed, by asking what the economy would have looked like without them. North argued that institutions — formal laws as well as informal norms — are what determine whether economies prosper or stagnate over the long run.

## 1994

The first prize for game theory — the study of decisions in which each person’s best choice depends on what others choose. John Nash defined the equilibrium named after him: a situation where no one can do better by changing their own choice alone. Reinhard Selten refined it to rule out threats that would never actually be carried out. John Harsanyi extended it to situations where people do not know everything about each other.

## 1995

Robert Lucas introduced rational expectations into macroeconomics: people use all the information they have, including what they know about government policy, when forming expectations about the future. One consequence, the “Lucas critique”, is that relationships estimated from past data can break down when policy changes, because people change their behaviour in response.

## 1996

James Mirrlees and William Vickrey studied how to design incentives when one side knows more than the other. Mirrlees worked out how progressive an income tax should be when the government can see people’s earnings but not their underlying ability. Vickrey showed that in a sealed-bid auction where the winner pays the second-highest bid, everyone’s best strategy is to bid what the item is truly worth to them. Vickrey died three days after the prize was announced.

## 1997

Robert Merton and Myron Scholes, building on work with Fischer Black, found a way to calculate the value of an option — a contract giving the right to buy or sell something later at a fixed price. Their formula made it possible to price and manage risk with derivatives, and helped financial markets in them grow rapidly. Black had died in 1995, and the prize is not awarded after death.

## 1998

Amartya Sen brought ethics back into economics. He showed that famines, including the Bengal famine of 1943, can happen without any overall fall in food supply, when particular groups lose the means to obtain food. He argued that well-being should be judged by what people are actually able to do and to be — their “capabilities” — rather than by income alone, an idea behind the United Nations’ Human Development Index. He is the first Indian-born economics laureate.

## 1999

Robert Mundell showed that a country cannot have a fixed exchange rate, free movement of capital and an independent monetary policy all at once; under a fixed rate, it is fiscal policy that works. He also asked when it makes sense for several regions or countries to share one currency — the theory of “optimum currency areas”, which laid the intellectual groundwork for the euro.

## 2000

James Heckman and Daniel McFadden developed methods for analysing data about individuals and households. Heckman tackled selection bias: wages are only observed for people who have chosen to work, so a simple average gives a misleading picture unless that choice is accounted for. McFadden built models of choices between a fixed set of options — whether to drive, take the bus or the train — which are now used to plan transport, energy and housing policy.

## 2001

George Akerlof, Michael Spence and Joseph Stiglitz showed how markets go wrong when one side knows more than the other. Akerlof’s “market for lemons” explains how buyers who cannot tell good used cars from bad drive good cars out of the market. Spence showed how the better-informed side can send a costly signal, such as an educational degree. Stiglitz showed how the less-informed side, such as an insurer or a bank, can design choices that sort people by their hidden characteristics.

## 2002

Daniel Kahneman, a psychologist, showed with Amos Tversky that people rely on mental shortcuts that produce predictable errors, and that they feel a loss more strongly than a gain of the same size; Tversky had died in 1996. Vernon Smith established laboratory experiments as a way of testing economic theory, showing in controlled trading sessions how different market rules shape the prices and quantities that emerge.

## 2003

Two methods for analysing data that move over time. Robert Engle developed a way to model volatility that changes over time — calm periods followed by turbulent ones — which is now standard in measuring financial risk. Clive Granger showed how to analyse series that drift without settling around an average, and introduced “cointegration”: two such series, like consumption and income, can still move together in the long run.

## 2004

Finn Kydland and Edward Prescott showed that a government free to change its mind later has an incentive to break its promises — for example, to allow more inflation than it pledged — so people do not believe it. This “time consistency” problem is a central argument for fixed rules and independent central banks. They also argued that business cycles can be driven by real shocks such as changes in technology, using methods now standard in macroeconomics.

## 2005

Robert Aumann and Thomas Schelling used game theory to understand conflict and cooperation. Schelling showed how commitments, credible threats and shared expectations shape bargaining, from nuclear deterrence to everyday negotiation. Aumann showed that people who deal with each other repeatedly can sustain cooperation even when each acts in their own interest, because the future gives them a reason to keep faith today.

## 2006

Edmund Phelps showed that the trade-off between inflation and unemployment holds only in the short run: once people come to expect higher inflation, unemployment returns to its underlying level, and only inflation stays higher. He also studied how much a society should save to give the highest sustainable consumption across generations.

## 2007

Leonid Hurwicz, Eric Maskin and Roger Myerson founded mechanism design, sometimes described as game theory in reverse. Instead of predicting what people will do under given rules, it starts from the outcome you want and asks how to design the rules — of an auction, a vote or a regulation — so that people acting in their own interest, and holding private information, produce it. Hurwicz, at 90, was then the oldest laureate.

## 2008

Paul Krugman explained why so much trade happens between similar countries in similar goods — cars traded for cars — through economies of scale and consumers’ taste for variety. He also explained why economic activity concentrates in a few places: firms gather where markets are large, and markets grow where firms gather. This became known as the “new economic geography”.

## 2009

Two answers to the question of who should govern economic activity. Elinor Ostrom showed that shared resources such as forests, fisheries and irrigation systems can be managed well by the communities that use them, without either privatisation or state control, and identified the rules that make this work. Oliver Williamson explained when activities are better organised inside a firm than through contracts in the market. Ostrom was the first woman to receive the prize.

## 2010

Peter Diamond, Dale Mortensen and Christopher Pissarides studied markets where buyers and sellers take time and effort to find each other — “search frictions”. Their model of the labour market explains why unemployment and job vacancies exist side by side, and how policies such as unemployment benefits affect how long people stay unemployed.

## 2011

Thomas Sargent and Christopher Sims developed ways to separate cause from effect in economy-wide data, where everything moves together. Sargent showed how to account for people’s expectations about policy, including why ending a hyperinflation requires a believable change of regime. Sims developed vector autoregressions, a method that traces how an economy responds over time to an unexpected shock, such as a surprise change in interest rates.

## 2012

Lloyd Shapley and Alvin Roth worked on markets where prices cannot do the matching — assigning doctors to hospitals, students to schools, or kidney donors to patients. Shapley, with David Gale, designed a procedure that produces stable matches, where no two parties would both rather be with each other. Roth put it into practice, redesigning the American medical residency match, school choice in New York and Boston, and kidney exchange programmes.

## 2013

A prize that held an apparent contradiction. Eugene Fama showed that share prices are very hard to predict over days and weeks, because markets absorb new information quickly. Robert Shiller showed that over several years they are more predictable, and swing more than their underlying value justifies. Lars Peter Hansen developed a statistical method for testing theories of asset prices like these.

## 2014

Jean Tirole studied how to regulate industries dominated by a few powerful firms, such as telecommunications, railways, banking and digital platforms. His central message is that simple rules applied to all industries work poorly, and that regulation should be designed for the conditions of each industry and for what the regulator can and cannot observe.

## 2015

Angus Deaton studied consumption — what households buy, and how it changes with income and prices — using detailed household surveys. His work shaped how poverty is measured in developing countries, including in India, and how well-being can be tracked through what people actually consume rather than through national averages.

## 2016

Oliver Hart and Bengt Holmström built contract theory. Holmström showed how to design pay when effort cannot be observed directly, by linking reward to every available signal of performance, not just the final result. Hart studied contracts that cannot foresee every situation, and showed that who holds ownership and decision rights then matters a great deal — with applications to whether public services, such as prisons, should be privatised.

## 2017

Richard Thaler built behavioural economics into the mainstream. He showed that people lack self-control, care about fairness, and treat money differently depending on which mental “account” it sits in. His work on “nudges” — changing the default choice, for example by enrolling workers in a pension plan automatically while letting them opt out — has been adopted by governments around the world.

## 2018

William Nordhaus built the first models linking the economy and the climate, which estimate the damage from carbon emissions and underpin the case for a carbon tax. Paul Romer explained long-run growth through ideas: because an idea can be used by everyone at once, investment in research drives growth, but markets left alone will produce too little of it.

## 2019

Abhijit Banerjee, Esther Duflo and Michael Kremer changed how anti-poverty policy is tested. They broke large questions into small, precise ones — does providing textbooks improve learning? does remedial teaching? — and answered them with randomised controlled trials, comparing groups chosen by lottery. Much of the work was done in India. Duflo was the second woman and the youngest person to receive the prize.

## 2020

Paul Milgrom and Robert Wilson developed auction theory, including how bidders behave when an item’s value is uncertain and the same for everyone, and why the winner often turns out to have overpaid — the “winner’s curse”. They then invented new auction formats for selling many related items at once, first used to sell radio frequencies in the United States in 1994 and since copied around the world, including for telecom spectrum.

## 2021

David Card used “natural experiments” — real events that happen to affect some people and not others — to study labour markets. His study with Alan Krueger, who died in 2019, found that a minimum wage rise in New Jersey did not reduce employment compared with neighbouring Pennsylvania. Joshua Angrist and Guido Imbens showed precisely what such experiments can and cannot tell us about cause and effect.

## 2022

Ben Bernanke, Douglas Diamond and Philip Dybvig explained why banks exist and why they are fragile. Diamond and Dybvig showed that banks turn short-term deposits into long-term loans, which makes them vulnerable to runs — and why deposit insurance prevents them. Bernanke showed that bank failures deepened the Great Depression by destroying lending relationships, and later, as chair of the Federal Reserve, led the response to the 2008 crisis.

## 2023

Claudia Goldin traced two centuries of women’s work in the United States. She showed that women’s participation in paid work fell with industrialisation before rising again, and that today most of the gap in pay between men and women arises within the same occupation, and opens up after the birth of a first child. She was the first woman to receive the prize unshared.

## 2024

Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson and James Robinson studied why some countries are rich and others poor. They argued that the difference lies in institutions: inclusive ones that protect property and spread power encourage investment, while extractive ones that serve a narrow elite do not. Using the history of European colonisation, they showed how institutions set up centuries ago still shape prosperity today.

## 2025

Three explanations of how economies grow without stopping. Joel Mokyr, an economic historian, showed that sustained growth since the Industrial Revolution required a steady flow of useful knowledge and a society open to change. Philippe Aghion and Peter Howitt built a model of “creative destruction”, in which new technologies and firms continually replace old ones, explaining how competition and innovation drive growth.

## 2026

Robert Hall and Michael Woodford were honoured for grounding macroeconomic stabilisation — the use of monetary and fiscal policy to smooth booms and recessions — in models of how individual households and firms behave. Hall’s work includes how household spending responds to changes in income and how wages and employment move over the business cycle. Woodford built the framework that most central banks now use to think about inflation targeting, and showed the power of managing expectations through clear communication about future policy.
