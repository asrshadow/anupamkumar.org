## The definition

Richard Musgrave, the German-born American economist whose 1959 treatise gave the subject
its modern shape, opened that book with a definition:

> "The complex of problems that center around the revenue-expenditure process of government
> is referred to traditionally as public finance."
>
> — Richard A. Musgrave, *The Theory of Public Finance: A Study in Public Economy*
> (New York: McGraw-Hill, 1959), p. 3.

He added at once that the money is not really the point. The flows are in rupees or
dollars, but the problems are real ones: how a country's resources are used, how income is
shared out, and whether the economy keeps everyone who wants work in work, with prices
steady. To keep these apart he imagined the government's fiscal department as three
branches, each with one job.

1. **Allocation.** Some things the market will not supply, or will supply too little of,
   because once they exist nobody can be stopped from using them: a streetlight, a flood
   embankment, clean air. Economists call these *public goods*. The allocation branch
   decides which of them the state provides, and raises taxes to pay for them.
2. **Distribution.** The market rewards people according to what they own and what they can
   sell. Society may judge that outcome unfair. The distribution branch adjusts it, through
   *progressive* taxes (a larger share taken from larger incomes) and *transfers* (money or
   goods given to those with less).
3. **Stabilisation.** Economies swing between booms and slumps. The stabilisation branch uses
   the budget to lean against the swing: spending more or taxing less in a slump, and the
   reverse in a boom.

Musgrave's insight was that these are three separate questions, even though a single budget
answers all of them at once. How many schools to build is not the same question as who
should pay for them, and neither is the same as whether this is a good year to borrow.
Most confused fiscal arguments are two of these questions pretending to be one.

## The same idea, for someone who has never noticed it

Suppose you are fifteen, and as far as you know, the government has never touched your
life. Walk through yesterday.

The streetlight was on when you left home. The bus drove on a road nobody you know paid to
build. Your school, its textbooks and perhaps its midday meal were paid for. The vaccines
you were given as a baby were free. None of this was free, of course. Somebody paid, and
the somebody was everyone, through taxes.

You paid too. Look at the bill for the packet of chips you bought on the way back. It shows
a line for GST, the Goods and Services Tax. That small amount went to the government. Every
packet, every phone recharge, every litre of petrol your family buys sends a share the same
way.

So money flows from everyone to the government, and from the government back out to roads,
schools, hospitals and much else. That two-way flow is what Musgrave called the
revenue-expenditure process. Public finance is the study of it: where the money comes from,
where it goes, and whether it was spent well.

Musgrave's three jobs are all there in your day:

- **The streetlight** is allocation. No single family would pay for the light on a whole
  street, so the government provides it for everyone.
- **The free textbooks and scholarships** are distribution. They make sure a child from a
  poor family starts closer to a child from a rich one.
- **What happened in 2020** is stabilisation. When COVID-19 shut the country down, the
  government spent heavily, on free grain for hundreds of millions of people among other
  things, to stop a sudden slump becoming a disaster.

You have not felt public finance because, when it works, it is invisible. You notice it
only when it fails: when the streetlight stays dark, or the road breaks up after one
monsoon. Learning to see it while it works is the first step to judging whether it is
working well.
