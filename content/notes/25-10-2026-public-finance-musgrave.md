## Who was Richard Musgrave

Richard Abel Musgrave (1910–2007) was born in Königstein, Germany, and moved to the United
States in 1933. He took his doctorate at Harvard, worked at the Federal Reserve Board in the
1940s, and taught at Michigan, Johns Hopkins, Princeton and Harvard before retiring to the
University of California, Santa Cruz. His seminal work, *The Theory of Public Finance*
(1959), set out the framework the subject still uses, and he is known as the father of
modern public finance. With his wife, the economist Peggy Musgrave, he also wrote *Public
Finance in Theory and Practice* (1973), the textbook from which generations of students
learned the field.

## The definition

Musgrave opened *The Theory of Public Finance* with this definition:

> "The complex of problems that center around the revenue-expenditure process of government
> is referred to traditionally as public finance."
>
> Richard A. Musgrave, *The Theory of Public Finance: A Study in Public Economy*
> (New York: McGraw-Hill, 1959), p. 3.

He went on to say that the money itself is not the main subject. The flows are counted in
rupees or dollars, but the problems behind them are about how a country uses its resources,
how income is shared out, and whether everyone who wants work can find it while prices stay
steady. To keep these problems apart, he pictured the government's fiscal department as
three branches, each with one job.

1. The *allocation* branch provides what the market will not supply, or will supply too
   little of, because once it exists nobody can be stopped from using it. A streetlight is
   the standard example. Economists call these *public goods*. This branch decides which of
   them the state provides, and raises taxes to pay for them.
2. The *distribution* branch adjusts who ends up with what. The market rewards people
   according to what they own and what they can sell, and society may judge that outcome
   unfair. This branch uses *progressive* taxes, which take a larger share from larger
   incomes, and *transfers*, which are money or goods given to people with less.
3. The *stabilisation* branch smooths out booms and slumps. In a slump it spends more or
   taxes less, and in a boom it does the reverse.

Musgrave argued that these are three separate questions, even though a single budget
answers all of them at once. How many schools to build is one question. Who should pay for
them is another. Whether this is a good year to borrow for them is a third. Fiscal debates
often go wrong because two of these get argued as if they were one.

## Public Finance in Daily Life

You may come from a poor family, a middle-class one or a rich one. You may or may not have
felt public finance at work in your own life, or ever stopped to think about it. So let us
look at a few ordinary situations.

The streetlight outside your home was on when you left in the morning. The bus you took ran
on a road that no family on your street paid to build. Your school, its textbooks and
perhaps its midday meal were paid for. The vaccines you were given as a baby cost your
parents nothing. All of these were paid for by everyone together, through taxes.

You pay tax too. Look at the bill for the packet of chips you bought on the way home. It
has a line for GST, the Goods and Services Tax, and that amount goes to the government. A
share of every phone recharge and every litre of petrol your family buys goes the same way.

Money flows from everyone to the government, and from the government back out to roads,
schools, hospitals and much else. Musgrave called this two-way flow the revenue-expenditure
process. Public finance studies it: where the money comes from, where it goes, and whether
it was spent well.

Which part you notice depends on where your family stands. A family that relies on a ration
shop meets public finance every month at the counter. A family that pays income tax sees it
deducted on every pay slip. Most families meet it in both ways, without calling it by name.

Musgrave's three branches are all in these situations:

- The streetlight is allocation. No single family would pay to light a whole street, so the
  government lights it for everyone.
- Free textbooks and scholarships are distribution. They help a child from a poor family
  start closer to a child from a rich one.
- The government's response to COVID-19 in 2020 was stabilisation. When the lockdown shut
  down work across the country, the government spent heavily, including on free grain for
  hundreds of millions of people, so that a sudden slump would not turn into a collapse.

When public finance works, few people notice it. They notice when it fails: when the
streetlight stays dark, or a new road breaks up after one monsoon. Seeing it while it works
is what lets you judge whether it is working well.
